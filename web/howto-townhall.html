Hold the meeting yourself

If the person who represents you will not meet the people they represent, you can hold the meeting anyway — and leave a chair empty for them.

The format is old, simple, and available to anybody. It belongs to no party: any constituency whose representative has stopped showing up can use it, and over the years plenty have, in both directions. What follows is how to run one so that it is a real meeting that someone declined to attend, rather than a piece of theatre about an empty chair.

1. Check this is actually the right tool

It is the right tool when someone has genuinely stopped holding open meetings. It is the wrong tool, and will rebound, if they hold them and you would rather not go.

  • Look first. Check their website, their newsletter and their office's events page. Telephone town halls and small invitation-only sessions are still meetings, even if they are not the ones you wanted.
  • Ask, in writing. Call or email the district office and ask when the next public meeting is. Keep the answer. If the answer is a real date, go to that instead — it will do more than anything on this page.
  • If there is no answer, or no date, you have the thing this format is for, and you have it in writing.

2. Invite them, and mean it

This is the step that makes the difference, and it is the one people skip.

Write to the official with a specific date, time and place, at least three weeks out, and offer to move it if the date does not suit. Say who is organising, roughly how many people you expect, and what the format will be. Ask for a reply by a named date.

Do this because you would genuinely rather they came. An event whose organisers hoped the guest would decline is a stunt, and it reads as one. An event whose organisers offered three dates and kept the replies is a meeting, and the empty chair means something because it did not have to be empty.

Keep every exchange. The invitation, the reply or the silence, and any alternative you offered. This is the whole evidential basis for the chair being empty, and later on somebody will ask.

3. The practical part

  • A room. Libraries, church halls, union halls, school gyms, community centres, fire halls. Ask about the fee and whether they require insurance — some do, and it is usually small but it is better not to find out three days before.
  • A date. Evening or weekend. Avoid the obvious clashes, including whatever your town cares about on a Friday night.
  • Two hours, and not more. An hour and a half is usually better.
  • Tell people. The local paper, community Facebook groups, neighbourhood apps, library and shop noticeboards, the newsletter of any group already involved. A week of notice is thin; two is comfortable.
  • Tell the official's office again, a week out. A reminder is not a gotcha, and it removes the “we never heard” response before it can be made.

4. Running it so it holds together

  • Someone chairs, and it is not the angriest person in the room. Their job is the clock and the queue, not the argument.
  • Announce the rules at the start and keep them the same for everybody: how long each person gets, two or three minutes, and whether questions are taken from a queue or from cards.
  • Someone takes notes. Name, question, and any answer if anyone present can give one.
  • Record it if you can, and say at the start that you are recording, so nobody is recorded unaware.
  • Leave the chair empty and leave it alone. The point is made by it being there. It does not need a sign, a costume or a joke, and every one of those turns the evening into a story about you rather than about them.
  • Collect contact details from anyone willing. The list of people who came is the most durable thing the evening produces.

5. Afterwards, which is where it works or doesn’t

The meeting is not the point. The record of it is.

  1. Write up the questions — just the questions, accurately, without commentary.
  2. Send them to the official with the number of people who attended and a request for written answers by a named date.
  3. Publish the same document. Your local paper will often take it. So will the community groups that helped promote it.
  4. Publish the answers when they come, in full, even if you disagree with them. Especially then.
  5. If they do not come, say so plainly, once, on the date you named. Then go back to the people on your list with the next thing.

What separates a meeting from a stunt

Only four things, and they are all things you control:

  • The invitation was real and you can show it.
  • The people were real — actual constituents, not a coach party.
  • The record is accurate, including the parts that do not flatter your side of it.
  • Nobody was ambushed. The official was invited in good time and knew what the format was.

Keep all four and the event survives being described honestly by somebody who wishes it had not happened — which is the only test of this kind of thing that matters.
