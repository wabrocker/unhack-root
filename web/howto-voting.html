Unhacking Democracy

in the United States

Knowledgeable, cross-partisan citizens teaming up to save Democracy.

i

Our mission is to educate citizens on how democratic systems have been exploited. We provide specific, actionable, cross-partisan ways to help repair them. That work rests on three things: knowledge you can verify, strength in numbers, and clarity about what each of our teams is for.

Our guide · about 20 minutes

Check your own vote, then five other people’s

Registrations get cancelled. Polling places move. Rules change between one election and the next. None of that is unusual, and almost nobody checks until the day, when it is too late to fix.

Everything below sends you to official sources — your state’s election office and federal government sites — and to nobody with a view about how you should vote. That is deliberate. This is about whether your vote works, not about what you do with it.

1. Check that you are registered, right now

Start at vote.gov, the federal government’s own registration site. Pick your state and it hands you straight to your state’s election office, which is the only place that actually knows.

Check the spelling of your name, your address, and your party affiliation if your state records one — in a closed primary state that field decides which ballot you get. If you have moved, married, divorced or changed your name since you last voted, assume something needs updating until you have seen otherwise.

2. Learn your three dates

Everybody knows the third one. It is the first that catches people.

  • The registration deadline. Some states cut off weeks before the election; some let you register on the day itself. Find yours.
  • When early or mail voting opens, and, for mail ballots, whether the deadline is when it is postmarked or when it arrives. That distinction decides elections and most people have never heard of it.
  • Election day, and the hours your polling place is open.

Put all three in your calendar with a week’s warning on the first two. That is the whole trick.

3. Know what your state asks for

Requirements differ considerably from state to state, and they change. Look up what yours currently asks — what identification, if any, and what counts as proof of address — on your state election office’s own site rather than from memory or from anyone else’s summary.

Then check your polling place, which is not necessarily where it was last time.

4. Know what happens if something goes wrong

This is the part almost nobody knows, and it is federal law that applies everywhere.

If your name is not on the list, or your eligibility is questioned, you can cast a provisional ballot. Section 302 of the Help America Vote Act of 2002 creates that right in every state. Your eligibility is then checked afterwards, and the jurisdiction is required to tell you whether your ballot was counted.

So: do not leave. Ask for a provisional ballot, cast it, and find out afterwards. Leaving because your name was missing is the one mistake that cannot be undone later.

The Election Assistance Commission — the federal agency that HAVA created — is the plainest source on this if you want it in the government’s own words.

5. Now do it for five other people

This is where the twenty minutes turns into something. Checking your own registration helps one person. Sending the link to five people who trust you helps five, and two of them will find something wrong.

Not a broadcast. Five individual messages to people who know you: family, a group chat, the people you work with. Something like — “I checked mine and found my address was out of date, so I’m sending this round. Takes two minutes: vote.gov.”

That works because it comes from you and because it asks for something small and specific. It has nothing to do with how anyone votes, which is also why nobody minds receiving it.

6. If you want to do more: work the polls

Poll workers are recruited, trained and paid by your county or your municipality, and there is a persistent shortage of them almost everywhere. It is one of the few pieces of civic work that both parties ask people to do, because elections do not run without it.

Your county’s election office handles applications. Search [your county] [your state] poll worker and prefer the .gov result.

The twenty-minute version

  1. Check your registration at vote.gov.
  2. Put your registration deadline in your calendar.
  3. Put early or mail voting dates in, with the postmark-or-arrival rule noted.
  4. Check what your state asks for, and where your polling place is.
  5. Remember: name not on the list means ask for a provisional ballot, not leave.
  6. Send it to five people, individually.
